SCA Golden Cup Standard: The Science of Coffee Brewing Ratios

The Specialty Coffee Association (SCA) Golden Cup Standard specifies that optimum coffee extraction requires a brew ratio between 1:15 and 1:18 (or 55 to 65 grams of ground coffee per 1 liter of fresh water). Within this targeted extraction window, dissolved flavor compounds—volatile organic acids, caramelized sugars, and complex aromatics—reach an optimal Total Dissolved Solids (TDS) concentration between 1.15% and 1.45%, preventing both harsh astringency (over-extraction) and sour hollowness (under-extraction).

1. The Fluid Dynamics of Blooming: Degassing Trapped CO2

Freshly roasted whole-bean coffee traps large quantities of carbon dioxide within its cellular cellulose matrix. When hot water first contacts freshly ground coffee, the gas rapidly expands and escapes. Introducing a 30 to 45-second bloom with approximately 2 to 2.5 times the coffee weight in water ensures that gas voids collapse. Without a proper bloom, escaping gases form hydrophobic barrier pockets that cause channeling, forcing bypass water through micro-cracks and yielding an uneven, bitter-sour extraction.

2. Diagnosing Extraction Defects: Over vs Under Extraction

If your cup does not taste balanced, refer to the diagnostic troubleshooting matrix below and adjust only one extraction variable at a time:

Sensory Symptom Probable Cause Corrective Action
Sharp, sour, vegetal, hollow finish Under-extraction (Inadequate solubles dissolved) Finer grind setting, increase water temperature by 1~2°C, or extend total contact time.
Drying mouthfeel, harsh bitterness, ashy aftertaste Over-extraction (Undesirable heavy tannins pulled) Coarser grind setting, lower water temperature, or shorten contact time.
Muddy texture, cloudy cup as it cools Excess fines clogging paper filter Calibrate burr alignment, sift excess micro-fines, or rinse paper filter prior to brewing.

3. Water Chemistry: The Solvent Behind Every Note

Because water accounts for more than 98% of your brewed cup, its mineral composition governs what flavor compounds are drawn into solution:

  • Ideal TDS (75 to 150 ppm): Water devoid of minerals (distilled or pure reverse osmosis) lacks the ionic affinity required to bind flavor compounds, resulting in flat and dull cups. Water exceeding 250 ppm masks delicate floral notes with chalky chalkiness.
  • Magnesium vs Calcium: Magnesium ions (Mg²⁺) strongly pull crisp fruit acids and aromatic monoterpenes, whereas Calcium ions (Ca²⁺) bond with creamy sweetness and viscous body.
  • Brewing Temperature Dynamics: Temperatures below 90°C (194°F) highlight delicate citric acid and brightness, whereas temperatures above 94°C (201°F) accelerate dissolution of caramelized sugars, body, and roasty notes.

4. Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

Q. Why did 320 ml of brew water produce only around 280 ml of coffee?

A. Coffee grounds physically retain approximately 2 grams of water per gram of dry coffee. For a 20g dose, approximately 40 ml of water stays trapped in the spent coffee bed as absorption yield loss.

Q. Should I always rinse my paper filter before brewing?

A. White oxygen-bleached filters have minimal paper taste, but unbleached brown kraft filters impart noticeable cardboard aromatics unless rinsed thoroughly with hot water prior to adding coffee grounds.

Q. Why does iced pour-over use a concentrated 1:10 ratio?

A. The hot extract drains directly onto 100~120g of ice cubes. Melting ice rapidly cools and dilutes the coffee to the perfect 1:15~1:16 drinking strength while trapping volatile aroma vapors that would otherwise evaporate.
